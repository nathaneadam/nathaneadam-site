Nobody is born a tech person.

This semester a student couldn't drag a folder from one place to another on her own laptop. She'd grown up on touchscreens, and nobody had ever made her do it.

I showed her how, gave her two other ways to do it, and told her to practice. That was the whole fix.

I see some version of this every semester. Maybe you've felt it too: a quiet sense that you're "not a tech person," and that the people who are got something you didn't. You didn't miss anything. A musician who never practiced scales can't play them either, and nobody calls that a personality trait.

Right now the AI conversation has split into two camps. One says it's going to save the world. The other says it's going to ruin it. Both camps skip the same step, which is sitting down and learning the thing.

Here's what I actually believe. Nearly everyone who uses a computer needs to keep learning what this magical device can do. AI is the newest tool on that list. It won't be the last one.

If you'd rather shoot on film or record to tape, I support you. Some great work still gets made that way. But if you want a career that lasts another twenty years, you have to keep choosing to learn the new tools as they show up.

I've had to make that choice myself, more than once.

Why I'm doing this

In 2001, digital was pulling the entertainment industry apart at the seams. Recording, editing, photography, file sharing, all of it at once. The million-dollar console was losing sessions to a $2,500 computer running Pro Tools LE, and people with twenty years of craft in their hands were being told the craft no longer counted.

It counted. It just needed somewhere new to live. That turned out to be the thing I spent the next 25 years teaching: over 6,000 students, one of the first online training companies, two books.

Now AI is doing to every career what digital did to entertainment.

So I'm doing what I did the last time. Figuring it out in the open, and teaching it as I go.

Let me be clear about what I'm not. I'm not a 25-year-old guru who cracked the code last Tuesday. I'm a professor with a doctorate in education, kids, a house, and a career I'm reinventing in public at an age where that's supposed to be a bad idea.

If I can do this at my age, so can you. Which is either encouraging or mildly insulting, depending on how you took it. :)

The longer version The credits

Twenty minutes, then twenty hours.

I'll admit something. I've copied prompts off the internet, pasted them into Claude or ChatGPT, and gotten back… a very confident wall of text I had no idea what to do with. That's the trap. Shiny object syndrome, with a new shiny object every Tuesday.

What actually works is slower and a lot less exciting. It's the same thing I've watched work in classrooms for twenty-five years:

  1. Spend 20 minutes to an hour on each of five different things.
  2. Give the one that grabbed you 20 hours. That's enough to get past the part where everything is confusing.
  3. Still enjoying it? Give it 100 hours over a month or two. Now you're good at it.
  4. Go to 2,000 hours only for the things you love, or the things the market is clearly asking for.

Why not just pick one lane and go all in? Some people should. After twenty-five years of teaching, my gut says about one student in twenty is what I call a 5%er. They know exactly what they want, and they'll put in the 10,000 hours to be the best at it. That's not research, by the way. That's a guy who has graded a lot of projects.

The rest of us are 80%ers. We like more than one thing. (Or, in my case, we can only stay interested in one thing for so long.) An 80%er can take those same 10,000 hours, put 2,000 into five different skills, and by my 80/20 math end up in the top 20% of each.

I watched this play out during COVID. One former student had spent twenty years building a great career in touring live sound, and only live sound. When the tours stopped, so did his work, for almost two years. Another former student had picked up engineering, shooting and editing video, some graphics, and system setup along the way. When the same shutdown hit, he moved into live streaming for churches and companies and had his best financial year ever.

So start with twenty minutes. If you want somewhere to begin, the diagnostic below takes about two. :)
